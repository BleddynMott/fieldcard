## 4. The design

### Every event: play, see your result, call everyone, see everyone

F4 and F6 point to one shape. After each event, you first see your own result against the truth. Then you make **one tap on the same board, about everyone**. Then everyone pours onto that board. The board is the graph, so there's nothing new to learn. That is Extraplot's mix (the truth, yours, everyone's) built from Perceptle's own events (A2). It still happens after each event, on the same board, not during play.

| event | play (Hands) | you see | then one tap, about everyone | everyone pours in | headline: what they called, then what happened |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **E1 Stop** | three goes at the notch | your three stops against the notch | on the still track: where did most people stop? | their stops pile up along the track | "Most called early. Most stopped late." |
| **E2 Count** | how many? (the dial) | the truth, 17, against your 15 | most people counted: too many / too few? | their answers as a hill on the number line | "Most called too many. Most counted too few." |
| **E3 Repeat** | tap the squares back | your run and where it ended | on the board: the square where most people went out | each square shaded by how many went out there | "Most went out on the corner." |
| **E4 Pair** | find the identical pair | the real pair, ringed | on the board: the shape that fooled most people | a blue ring on each shape, sized by how many fell for it | "Most blamed the triangles. It was the blue squares." |
| **E5 Sort** | five blobs in size order | the true order | the blob most people put first | each blob filled by how many put it first | "Most thought the long one was biggest." |

Why the headline is two halves: "what everyone called" is Extraplot's "everyone's guesses" layer, the one cut in round 6 (`G-02-drift.md` cause 1). Putting it in words keeps the picture to four marks, and keeps the layer.

**Grounds for each call** (F2): you've just played the same board, you've seen your own slip, and you've seen the truth. The call asks whether your slip was everyone's.

### Making the events better: a trap on some days

From the fresh agent (section 8). The reveal teaches something (A3) only if the crowd goes wrong in an interesting way. So on some days, the day's content holds one known trap:
- Count: a colour close to today's.
- Pair: a near-identical decoy.
- Sort: a long, thin blob that looks bigger than it is.
- Stop: the marker eases in before the notch.
- Repeat: a square that comes up twice.

Two kinds of day keep the calls from being solved by "it's always the trap": days with no trap, and days where the trap fails to fool anyone. This is the change to the games themselves that his note asked for.

## 5. The card for one day (recommended)

1. **Home.** The play button, and what settled overnight (step 6).
2. **Five events.** Each: play → your result → one tap about everyone → everyone pours in. One day a week, a twist takes one event's tap.
3. **Claim it.** Plant your flag where your play finished among everyone. It isn't a sum: the five events score differently, so you judge it by feel from what you've seen. The reveal is the psychology: most people still aim high, even after seeing their slips.
4. **Hot Seat.** "Bristol planted at 90. Made it?" You see their flag and their record of past claims (✓✗ dots, from 1b), not their results, so it's a read of the person: do they bluff? Your grounds are that you've just played the same card and know how hard 90 is today. **Your own flag goes into other players' Hot Seats.**
5. **Scorecard.** Hands and Hunch. Everything is "so far" and settles at midnight (A5).
6. **Tomorrow's home.** What settled; and "22 of 37 believed you", a row of filled and empty figures. Strangers judged you overnight (A5, test 4).
7. **Sunday.** Your week as a character, and the event that floored the most people.

Length, estimated and not measured: about 30 s per event (play, result, a 3 s tap, the pour), plus about 30 s for steps 3 to 5. **About 3 min**, against Perceptle's 2.

## 6. Graphs, done properly (rules for every reveal)

1. The graph is the board you just played, or one number line. Never a new chart.
2. Four marks at most, the same everywhere:
   - the truth, in ink: a line, or a ringed answer;
   - everyone's play, in blue;
   - you, a yellow dot;
   - your call, a yellow pin. It is a pin, not a ring, so it can't be confused with E4's crowd rings.
   
   Everyone's calls go in the headline, not on the picture.
3. It builds in order: the truth and you first, then your pin, then everyone pours in.
4. No legend, no second axis, no arrows, no tick-and-cross panels, no columns of 100 dots.
5. The headline says the one thing. On trap days, the "Why?" chip holds one line of why (G-00b G5).
6. Claim it's reveal follows the same rules: one track, everyone's flags above, their finishes below, yours in yellow. Headline: "Most aimed too high."

**Clashes with `design/DESIGN.md`, named for his ruling:**
- Don't 2 (no more than two data series) is broken by rule 2.
- Do 2 (never a claim about why) is broken by rule 5.

Both are the cause-1 removals in `G-02-drift.md`. If he picks this design, both lines change in the same task.

## 7. Open tensions and risks, most likely killer first

1. **The calls become a formality.** Each call has one tap and points riding on it. If the answer is guessable ("it's always the trap"), the five calls are a quiz bolted on: the "Perceptle with a quiz" of test 6. What answers it: days with and without traps; a headline that changes daily; the believers count. Whether that's enough only shows in play, so a tappable prototype is the next check.
2. **Flat crowds.** If everyone lands on the truth, the reveal teaches nothing. Traps help, but need real players to confirm.
3. **Thin crowds early.** With 30 players the pictures are noise. A reveal needs a minimum crowd, or pooled data, before it shows.
4. **Delete test** (test 1). Strictly, removing the five taps leaves the events as a whole game, so it fails as worded. Making Hunch the headline score would pass it. His call.
5. **Five calls a day, against "not repeated 5 times".** They are five different questions on five boards. The lighter fallback is three a day, rotating, with everyone still poured onto all five boards.
6. **Length.** About 3 min, against Perceptle's 2.

### B3. Why round 1 is boring

Round 1 made the crowd an echo of the task: play it, then play it again "as everyone". In that design:
- **Nothing is at stake.** A wrong tap costs a few points, and nothing is risked by choice. That drops his high-risk, high-reward note.
- **Nobody is there.** The crowd is a shading on your own board. There's no person, side or rival.
- **There's no strategy.** The call never changes what you do, so the best call is always your honest guess.
- **There's no surprise about you.** The reveal is about everyone, never "you did the same thing".
- **Every day is the same.** It drops his distribution variants day to day.

So round 2 judges every concept on five things the echo lacks:
- **stakes** (a choice with something on it);
- **people** (real others, sides);
- **strategy** (the crowd changes what you should do);
- **surprise about you**;
- **variety** (the day's form changes).

The common-sense filters F1 to F5 still hold.

### B5. Converged: five directions (studio stage 3; he picks)

Each direction is a different bet, not a variant of round 1. Each is checked against B3 (stakes, people, strategy, surprise about you, variety) and F1 to F5.

**D1. Against the pile: the crowd is on the board while you play.** There is no guess step after the event. Reading the crowd is how you play.
- Count: your answer line is everyone's earlier answers, as a pile. Trust your eye or the pile. A right answer pays more when fewer were right.
- Stop: land where nobody has stopped yet. Empty spots are hard to hit, so this answers "too easy".
- Repeat: push your luck, round by round, against the crowd's survival curve.
- Pair: four real players' picks are shown. Hold or cave.
- Sort: race the real pack of earlier finishes.

The distribution is the playing field, so his variants (placing yourself, a real distribution) are on every board. B3: stakes ✓, people ✓ (real earlier players), strategy ✓, surprise ✓ ("71% didn't trust their eye"), variety ✓ (five rules). It also passes the delete test (test 1): take the crowd away and the events change. Risk: five different crowd rules to learn. Players arriving early see a thin pile.

**D2. Poker face: your flag can lie.** Claim it becomes a bluff. Plant your flag honestly or higher. Then call three strangers' flags, true or bluff, from their record of past claims. Tomorrow you see "22 of 37 believed you". A lie that's believed pays big, a caught lie costs, the truth pays a little, and a lie pays more when fewer believe it. It joins Claim it, the Hot Seat and "where people really finished vs where they thought" into one moment, with real people and a next-day result. The weekly personality is Honest, Chancer or Bluffer. Risk: on its own it leaves the five events plain, the flat version. It needs D1 or D4 alongside.

**D3. Nerve: your day's score is at risk.** Your Hands score goes on the rope. Hold while the crowd lets go: the fewer still holding, the more it pays, until it snaps. This is his "high risk high reward ... for your personal record", never built. The reveal is one curve: the crowd letting go, the snap, your release. Risk: if the snap is arbitrary, it's luck. It has to be tied to the day's crowd so it shifts daily.

**D4. The lab: each day, one event runs an experiment on everyone.** The experiments rotate: anchoring (a bib number before Count), conformity (The Four), framing (Twin Heats), overconfidence (Bracket). The reveal: "a bib number moved everyone's count by 15 — and yours by 9". Sunday is your profile, the weekly personality he called "cool": Anchored, Bold, Loner. This is the strongest "social experiment vibe" and the most repeatable lines. Risk: a trick stops working once known, so effects must rotate and vary in strength.
